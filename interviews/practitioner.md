# Interview Notes: Practitioner (Chief People Officer)

> **Illustrative composite.** Not a real individual. Constructed to reflect positions documented in the published debate, especially the practitioner-beliefs evidence in Rynes et al. (see `documents/`). Conducted by the engagement team, 40 minutes.

**Role:** Chief People Officer at a ~6,000-employee technology company; MBA from a research school; describes herself as "the most research-friendly executive I know."

## Key Points

On consumption:
- "I *want* what you have. I make hundred-million-dollar decisions about hiring, teams, incentives, and change, there are literally journals full of evidence about all four. I have read maybe five academic papers in ten years, and two of those were because a professor friend emailed me a PDF."
- Why: "I tried. I once had a real question about pay transparency and found a paper that looked perfect. Paywall, forty dollars. Fine, I paid. Then it's forty pages, and the answer to my question lives in a three-way interaction in Table 6 with a paragraph of hedges. I have thirty minutes. McKinsey gives me a chart and a claim. The chart is probably half-wrong, but I can *use* it in Tuesday's meeting."

On the intermediary market:
- Her actual research diet: HBR, two newsletters, LinkedIn, conference talks, and vendor white papers. "Do you know who reads your journals? The consultants. They read you, water it down, rebrand it, and sell it back to me at two thousand dollars an hour. The translation layer exists, you just gave the margin away."
- On evidence quality: knows some of what she consumes is unreliable. "I'd pay real money for a source with academic credibility at practitioner speed. That product doesn't exist."

On what would work:
- One-page evidence summaries per management question ("What do we actually know about X"), written for decision-makers, updated as evidence accumulates, stamped by AOM, "the Cochrane thing doctors have. Why don't you have that?"
- "Stop asking me to come to your conference. Come to mine."

## Read Between the Lines (engagement team notes)

Demand exists, this is the *most* favorable practitioner segment, and even she consumes zero primary literature. The failure is format, speed, access, and channel, not interest. Two product-shaped leads: (1) the "Cochrane for management" evidence-summary product; (2) reclaiming the translation margin currently captured by consultancies and HBR. Note also the accessibility detail: the paywall is a real but secondary barrier, the forty *pages* were a bigger obstacle than the forty dollars.
