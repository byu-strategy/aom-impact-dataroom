# The Publisher Oligopoly: Who Controls Scholarly Output

**Full citation:** Larivière, Haustein & Mongeon, 2015, The Oligopoly of Academic Publishers in the Digital Era, PLOS ONE 10(6): e0127502, doi 10.1371/journal.pone.0127502.

**Why it's in this data room:** This is the hard evidence on publisher market concentration, based on 45 million documents indexed in the Web of Science from 1973 to 2013. It grounds the case's monopoly and market-structure theme with data rather than opinion, showing exactly how much of the scholarly record, especially in the social sciences, is controlled by five commercial firms, and how profitable that control has become.

## Core Argument

Larivière and colleagues show that the digital transition, which many hoped would democratize scholarly communication, instead accelerated consolidation. Since the mid-1990s, five publishers (Reed-Elsevier, Wiley-Blackwell, Springer, Taylor & Francis, and either the American Chemical Society or Sage depending on domain) have absorbed a rising share of published papers through journal acquisitions and new journal creation. Concentration is most extreme in the social sciences, where scientific societies are small, fragmented, and often unable to manage the shift to electronic publishing on their own. Because authors, reviewers, and readers all contribute labor without compensation, and because each paper is a unique good with no substitute, publishers extract profit margins that exceed those of the most profitable pharmaceutical, banking, and auto companies. The authors conclude by questioning what value publishers now add, and note that evaluation systems built on high-impact commercial journals reinforce the oligopoly.

## Key Evidence & Quotes

- "Combined, the top five most prolific publishers account for more than 50% of all papers published in 2013. Disciplines of the social sciences have the highest level of concentration (70% of papers from the top five publishers)" (p. 1).
- "In the SSH, the concentration increased even more dramatically. Between 1973 and 1990, the five most prolific publishers combined accounted for less than 10% of the published output of the domain" (p. 5).
- "since then, this share has increased to more than 51%, meaning that, in 2013, the majority of SSH papers are published by journals that belong to five commercial publishers" (p. 5).
- "while the top five publishers accounted for 15% of papers in 1995, this value reached 66% in 2013. Combined, the top three commercial publishers alone—Reed-Elsevier, Taylor & Francis and Wiley-Blackwell—represent almost 50% of all papers in 2013" (p. 7, on social science disciplines).
- "While profits more than doubled over that period—from 665M USD to 1,451M USD—profit margin also rose from 17% to 26%" (p. 10, on Reed-Elsevier, 1991-2013).
- "putting them on a comparable level with Pfizer (42%), the Industrial & Commercial Bank of China (29%) and far above Hyundai Motors (10%)" (p. 10, on publisher profit margins).
- "Unlike usual suppliers, authors provide their goods without financial compensation and consumers (i.e. readers) are isolated from the purchase" (p. 11).
- "publishing in a high impact Elsevier or Springer journal is what 'counts'" (p. 13).

## Relevance to the Engagement

AOM holds an unusual structural advantage: it owns its journals (AMJ, AMR, AMP, and siblings) rather than having ceded the titles to a commercial house, as happened across much of the social sciences documented here. That ownership is leverage. Where fields locked into Elsevier or Wiley portfolios must negotiate access, pricing, and reuse rights with an oligopolist, AOM can choose its own dissemination model, including open-access routes, practitioner-facing translation layers, and open-research infrastructure, without asking a shareholder-driven intermediary for permission. The paywall question, a first-order barrier to practitioner impact, is largely within the Board's own control.

## Both/And Note

Nothing in this evidence argues against rigorous peer review; the authors themselves note that quality control comes from the scholarly community, not from publishers. The both/and move is to keep the rigor the community already supplies while redesigning distribution so rigorous work actually reaches managers. Market structure, not scholarly standards, is the constraint this document targets.
