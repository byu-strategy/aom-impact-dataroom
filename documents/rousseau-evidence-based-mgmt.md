# Rousseau (2006): Is There Such a Thing as "Evidence-Based Management"?

**Full citation:** Rousseau, 2006, Is There Such a Thing as "Evidence-Based Management"?, Academy of Management Review 31(2): 256-269, doi 10.5465/amr.2006.20208679.

**Why it's in this data room:** This is a second AOM presidential address (Denise Rousseau's 2005 address, delivered as the Academy's sixtieth president) diagnosing the research-practice gap, which signals that the client's leadership has repeatedly named impact as an unsolved institutional problem. Unlike purely diagnostic treatments, Rousseau offers a constructive program: evidence-based management (EBM), modeled explicitly on evidence-based medicine, as the bridge between rigorous scholarship and managerial practice.

## Core Argument

Rousseau argues that organizational research holds a largely unfulfilled promise for improving management practice. Managers rarely consult research; they rely on personal experience, guru advice, and fads, so practices of known effectiveness go unused while ineffective ones persist. Her answer is evidence-based management: translating cause-effect principles from social science into organizational practices, combining "Big E Evidence" (generalizable scientific knowledge) with "little e evidence" (local facts). Medicine shows this can be institutionalized through professional training, evidence-review communities such as the Cochrane Collaboration, and decision supports. Crucially, Rousseau assigns much of the blame not to managers but to academia itself, especially educators, and closes with concrete roles for researchers, educators, and managers in building translation infrastructure.

## Key Evidence & Quotes

- Definition of EBM (p. 256): "Evidence-based management means translating principles based on best evidence into organizational practices. Through evidence-based management, practicing managers develop into experts who make organizational decisions informed by social science and organizational research"
- The gap defined (p. 256, abstract): the prevailing "'research-practice gap'—the failure of organizations and managers to base practices on best available evidence"
- The verdict on the title question (p. 258): "The result is a research-practice gap, indicating that the answer to this article's title question is no—at least not yet."
- The medicine exemplar (p. 258): "Medicine is a success story as the first domain to institutionalize evidence-based practice. Evidence-based medicine is the integration of individual clinical expertise and the best external evidence."
- Baseline evidence of non-use (p. 261): "Less than 1 percent of HR managers read the academic literature regularly (Rynes, Brown, & Colbert, 2002), and the consultants who advise them are unlikely to do so either."
- Academia's own culpability (p. 262): "We typically do not educate managers to know or use scientific evidence."
- The infrastructure prescription (p. 267): "We need knowledge-sharing networks composed of educators, researchers, and manager/practitioners to help create and disseminate management-oriented research summaries and practices that best evidence supports."
- Guardrail against misuse (p. 267): "Evidence-based practice is not one-size-fits-all; it's the best current evidence coupled with informed expert judgment."

## Relevance to the Engagement

Rousseau supplies three assets for this engagement. First, measurement baselines: the "less than 1 percent" HR readership figure and documented persistence of ineffective practices (downsizing, discredited theories in textbooks) give the team quantifiable starting points for an impact scorecard. Second, a translation-infrastructure blueprint: Cochrane-style evidence-review collaboratives, practice-oriented evidence summaries, decision supports, and checklists are concrete deliverables AOM could sponsor rather than aspirations. Third, the education channel: Rousseau locates high leverage in business schools, where curricula, textbooks, and case teaching rarely model evidence use, making faculty and doctoral training a primary intervention point the Board directly influences.

## Both/And Note

Rousseau's program is not a critique of rigor; it presumes rigor, since only strong evidence is worth translating, and "strong evidence trumps weak" (p. 258). The failure she identifies is in translation and education, not in research quality. EBM thus models the both/and stance this engagement takes: keep the science, build the bridge.
