# Progress Since Hambrick (1994): An Honest Scorecard

Hambrick asked "What if the Academy actually mattered?" from the AOM presidential podium in August 1993. This note inventories what has actually changed in the 32 years since, so the engagement team neither overclaims stasis nor overclaims progress. Verify specifics before quoting to the Board; items below are directionally solid but compiled from public knowledge, not a systematic audit.

## Real Progress (things that exist now that did not in 1994)

**1. Accreditation now names societal impact.** AACSB's 2020 accreditation standards made "positive societal impact" an explicit expectation across a school's research, teaching, and outreach. This is a genuine scorecard change at the institutional level: deans must now write impact narratives for accreditors. (See the dean interview: whether it has reached individual P&T decisions is another matter.)

**2. National research assessment pays for impact (outside the US).** The UK's Research Excellence Framework has required "impact case studies" since 2014, weighted at roughly a fifth to a quarter of a department's assessment, with funding consequences. Australia and the Netherlands run analogous exercises. UK and European business schools now hire and promote partly on demonstrable impact. The US has no equivalent, which is why the equilibrium described in the interviews is most intact in US research schools.

**3. AOM built practice-facing and phenomenon-facing products.** Academy of Management Perspectives (practice-oriented translation), Academy of Management Discoveries (founded mid-2010s for phenomenon-driven work that classic theory-first review would screen out), and AOM Insights (a translational summary platform launched circa 2019-20 that turns journal articles into short practitioner briefs). The product line Hambrick implicitly asked for partially exists.

**4. Organized reform movements with elite backing.** Evidence-based management (Rousseau's 2006 presidential address; the Center for Evidence-Based Management), RRBM (2017 position paper, hundreds of senior signatories, AACSB partnership), and the broader responsible-research agenda. Diagnosis has become coalition.

**5. Open-science infrastructure reached management.** Preprints and working-paper norms (SSRN), open data and materials badges, registered reports at a growing set of journals, large-scale replication conversations, and DORA (the 2013 declaration moving institutions away from journal-level metrics). Management lags psychology here but the direction is real.

**6. The translation market exploded, with scholars in it.** A visible cohort of management scholars now reaches practitioners at scale through trade books, TED, LinkedIn, newsletters, and podcasts. The clearest research-to-practice win: psychological safety, an ASQ-grade research stream that a major tech company's internal team-effectiveness study made a household management term, now embedded in corporate practice worldwide. People-analytics functions staffed by PhDs, evidence-informed hiring practices (structured interviews, work samples), and goal-setting lineages in OKR practice are similar diffusion stories.

## What Has Not Moved

**1. The core currency.** At US research schools, tenure still turns on A-journal counts, essentially as in 1994. The FT50/UTD lists hardened the currency rather than loosening it. (Heckman and Moktan document the same dynamic in economics.)

**2. Direct practitioner consumption.** Managers still do not read the journals; the Rynes et al. line of evidence on practitioner belief gaps has not shown a reversal. The intermediary markup (consultancies, HBR, vendors) still captures the translation value.

**3. Measurement.** There is still no agreed impact KPI for management scholarship (Aguinis et al. 2014 proposed pluralist measurement; nobody operationalized it at scale). The Board cannot state its own baseline. Thirty-two years of debate produced no instrument.

**4. The incentive for the young.** The PhD-student calculus (see interview) is unchanged: impact work remains a post-tenure luxury good, which means the equilibrium reproduces itself with each cohort.

## The Pattern, For Your Issue Tree

Where change happened, it came from **scorecard-holders and infrastructure** (accreditors, national assessment regimes, new journals and platforms, open-science tooling), or from **market channels that bypass the journals entirely** (books, tech-company diffusion, people analytics). Where change did not happen, the mechanism on offer was **exhortation** (addresses, editorials, position papers). Thirty-two years of natural experiment, one lesson: speeches diagnose, scorecards and products move. Recommend accordingly.
