# The Wider Debate, Annotated Map

The six full summaries in this folder (Akerlof, Heckman, Hambrick, Ghoshal, Rynes et al., Larivière et al.) are the anchors. This file maps the rest of the published debate so you (and your AI) can go deeper. All metadata verified via Crossref, July 2026.

## The Field's Own Reckoning

- **Pfeffer & Fong (2002), "The End of Business Schools? Less Success Than Meets the Eye," *AMLE***, doi:10.5465/amle.2002.7373679. Data-driven skepticism that b-school research (and the MBA) influences practice; 1,270+ citations.
- **Bennis & O'Toole (2005), "How Business Schools Lost Their Way," *Harvard Business Review*, May 2005**, the critique in the practitioners' own magazine: the "scientific model" displaced the professional-school model.
- **Tourish (2020), "The Triumph of Nonsense in Management Studies," *AMLE***, doi:10.5465/amle.2019.0255. The modern sharp restatement: theory fetishism and unreadable prose as career strategy.
- **Tsui (2013), "On Compassion in Scholarship: Why Should We Care?" *AMR* (2012 presidential address)**, the address that seeded RRBM. A third AOM president on this same theme.

## The Rigor–Relevance Debate Proper

- **Rousseau (2006), "Is There Such a Thing as 'Evidence-Based Management'?" *AMR* (presidential address)**, doi:10.5465/amr.2006.20208679. Full summary of the paper is worth reading (PDF in the engagement library); the EBM program with medicine as exemplar.
- **Van de Ven & Johnson (2006), "Knowledge for Theory and Practice," *AMR***, doi:10.5465/amr.2006.22527385. Engaged scholarship: co-producing knowledge with practitioners as a *method*, not a compromise.
- **Kieser & Leiner (2009), "Why the Rigour–Relevance Gap in Management Research Is Unbridgeable," *JMS***, doi:10.1111/j.1467-6486.2009.00831.x. **The null hypothesis of this engagement.** Systems-theory argument that science and practice are self-referential systems that cannot directly communicate; "relevance" initiatives produce only rituals. If your recommendation can't answer Kieser & Leiner, it isn't ready for the Board.
- **Hodgkinson & Rousseau (2009), "…It's Already Happening!" *JMS***, doi:10.1111/j.1467-6486.2009.00832.x. The direct rebuttal, same issue. Read the pair.
- **Rynes, Colbert & Brown (2002), "HR Professionals' Beliefs…," *Human Resource Management***, doi:10.1002/hrm.10029. The empirical smoking gun: large, measurable gaps between practitioner beliefs and research consensus, the closest thing to a baseline impact measurement the field has.

## Incentives, Metrics & Market Structure

- **Edwards & Roy (2017), "Academic Research in the 21st Century…Perverse Incentives and Hypercompetition," *Environmental Engineering Science***, doi:10.1089/ees.2016.0223. Goodhart's law applied to science careers; open access.
- **Bergstrom (2001), "Free Labor for Costly Journals?" *JEP***, doi:10.1257/jep.15.4.183. The economics of publisher rent extraction from unpaid academic labor.
- **Alvesson & Sandberg (2011), "Ways of Constructing Research Questions: Gap-Spotting or Problematization?" *Organization***, doi:10.1177/1350508410372151. Why incentives produce incremental gap-filling instead of important questions, Akerlof's argument, management edition, four years earlier.

## The Constructive Program

- **George, Howard-Grenville, Joshi & Tihanyi (2016), "Understanding and Tackling Societal Grand Challenges through Management Research," *AMJ***, doi:10.5465/amj.2016.4007. Full summary in this folder's library; the field's aspirational redirection.
- **Wickert, Post, Doh, Prescott & Prencipe (2020), "Management Research that Makes a Difference," *JMS***, doi:10.1111/joms.12666. Broadens what "impact" means and how to evidence it.
- **Aguinis, Shapiro, Antonacopoulou & Cummings (2014), "Scholarly Impact: A Pluralist Conceptualization," *AMLE***, doi:10.5465/amle.2014.0121. Directly on the Board's measurement question: impact is plural (theory, practice, teaching, policy) and mono-metric evaluation miscounts it.
- **RRBM Position Paper**, see `rrbm-summary.md`.

## The Acemoglu Defense (the objection someone in the room will raise)

The strongest defense of the status quo is not "the gap doesn't exist," it is a career-sequencing argument: *do rigorous work for seven years, earn tenure, and tenure buys you the freedom to do impact work for the remaining thirty.* Exhibit A is usually Daron Acemoglu: decades of technical economics, then *Why Nations Fail* (2012), *Power and Progress* (2023), global policy influence, and the 2024 Nobel. If the sequencing model works, the system needs no repair, just patience. Kieser & Leiner (above) is the null hypothesis at the system level; this is the null hypothesis at the career level. Take it seriously, then check it against the record:

- **The exhibit cuts the other way.** Acemoglu's impact work is not a post-tenure pivot to a new activity; it is a translation of the same research program that earned tenure. **Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson (2001), "The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development," *American Economic Review***, doi:10.1257/aer.91.5.1369, one of the most-cited economics papers ever written, *is* the tenure-track work and *is* the substrate of *Why Nations Fail*. He never sequenced rigor and importance; he chose a first-order question and was rigorous about it from year one. In Akerlof's terms (see `akerlof-sins-of-omission.md`), he optimized importance *and* hardness, which is precisely what the standard advice to junior scholars (pick the clean gap-filling play, defer the question you care about) tells them not to do. Compare Alvesson & Sandberg (above) and the PhD-student interview.
- **The incentive gradient does not end at tenure.** Promotion to full, chaired positions, editorships, mobility, and salary reviews still count A-hits and count practical impact at roughly zero; see the dean interview ("a tiebreaker that has never broken a tie"). Tenure lengthens the leash, it does not change the scorecard.
- **The base rate is the evidence.** AOM has ~18,000 members, the senior cohort tenured for decades. If deferred impact worked at scale, the Rynes et al. practitioner-gap findings would not look the way they do. The PhD-student interview states the mechanism: "Everyone says 'after tenure.' I've started noticing how few people I can name who actually did it after tenure. The habits are the person by then."
- **The path ran on infrastructure management lacks.** Economics has ungated NBER working papers that journalists and policymakers read, Brookings, VoxEU, and trade publishers who treat economists as public intellectuals. A tenured management scholar who wakes up wanting impact has HBR and little else. The reproducible part of the Acemoglu path is the translation plumbing, and building that plumbing is an association-level (i.e., AOM-level) act, which is why this objection lands inside the engagement rather than against it.
- **Survivorship.** Designing a field's incentive advice around its single most extreme outlier is the move the diagnosis task asks you to catch. And note that Akerlof and Heckman, the two economists anchoring this data room, are insiders arguing that even economics, with all that infrastructure and all those public intellectuals, suffers sins of omission and top-five tyranny. The field where the sequencing model works best still indicts it from within.

## A Note on Interdisciplinarity (a live sin of omission)

One pattern cuts across this whole map: the highest-impact recent research on management phenomena is increasingly done by *other disciplines*. The landmark field experiment on AI and consulting work (758 BCG consultants, the "jagged frontier") was led substantially by economists and reached the world through *Science*-adjacent and NBER channels; the fast-growing literature on generative AI and occupations is dominated by economics and computer science, with management's organizational-theory contribution comparatively thin and late. Meanwhile the field's grand-challenges agenda (George et al. 2016) and RRBM principle 6 both say impact-relevant problems are inherently multidisciplinary. Diagnostic question for the engagement: is the field's incentive system (theory-contribution gatekeeping, discipline-bound journals, A-list currency) structurally filtering out the problem-driven, cross-disciplinary work through which fields earn public relevance? If economists can run experiments inside consulting firms and publish them to global attention, the sites and questions were available. The field chose its journals instead.

## How to Use This Map

The debate has run for 30+ years with remarkable continuity: Hambrick (1994) → Rynes et al. (2001) → Ghoshal (2005) → Rousseau (2006) → Kieser & Leiner (2009) → George et al. (2016) → RRBM → Tourish (2020) → Akerlof/Heckman (2020, from economics). Two facts about that arc matter for your recommendation: (1) diagnosis is not the bottleneck, the field has diagnosed itself continuously and eloquently; (2) every wave produced exhortation, and exhortation has not moved the scorecards (see the dean interview). Whatever you recommend, make it a *mechanism*, not a speech.
