# The Global Research Impact Framework (2026)

**Full citation:** AACSB Global Research Impact Task Force. 2026. *A Framework for Research Impact: Insights, Pathways, and Calls to Action.* Tampa, FL: AACSB International, May 2026. 46 pp. Released 26 May 2026 at the inaugural AOM/AACSB Research Impact Conference, University College Dublin.

**Full text is in this data room:** `documents/a-framework-for-research-impact-2026.pdf`. Read it.

**Why it's in this data room:** This is the engagement's starting condition, not background reading. The client co-authored it. AOM was one of ten partner organizations on the task force, and both AOM's current President (Tammy L. Madsen) and its immediate Past President (Peter Bamberger) are named members. The field's leadership has already committed, publicly and in writing, to a broader definition of research impact. The Board is no longer asking whether to act. It is asking what AOM does next.

## What the Framework Says

Ten scholarly and accrediting organizations spent 2025 asking how business school research impact should be defined, measured, and advanced. Their answer, in five moves:

1. **Impact is broader than citations.** "High-quality research is defined not solely by its rigor but also by its relevance, resonance, intellectual contribution, and capacity to generate meaningful impact. These dimensions are complementary rather than competing." (p. 12)
2. **Impact is nonlinear.** It emerges through engagement, collaboration, and translation throughout the research process, not at the end of it.
3. **The school, not the scholar, is the unit of analysis.** Schools should run portfolio-based impact strategies across three channels: scholarly discovery, teaching, and external engagement.
4. **Current incentives are the binding constraint.** Evaluation remains "overly concentrated on quantitative and journal-based metrics, limiting broader forms of impactful and externally engaged scholarship." (p. 5)
5. **No one can move alone.** Progress requires coordinated action across accreditors, associations, journals, publishers, rankings, funders, practitioners, policymakers, and schools.

It ships two operational artifacts: a **Research Impact Assessment Tool** (Appendix B, the "spiral": Insight, Input, Outputs, Outcomes, Impact, each with indicators, advantages, and limitations) and a **Research Impact Ecosystem Guide** (seven levers: Strategic Intent, Governance and Guardrails, Capacity and Incentives, Impact Portfolio, Activation Pathways, Measurement and Feedback, Amplification and Influence).

## Key Evidence & Quotes

- "This report is a foundation, not a conclusion. The next phase of work focuses on implementation: deeper collaboration, sharper commitments, and coordinated action to drive sectorwide change." (p. 34)
- "No institution can do this alone. Meaningful change depends on the collective efforts of the entities identified below. Each has a role. Each has leverage." (p. 34)
- 87% of deans and 82% of faculty "somewhat or strongly agree that business schools should broaden their definition of impactful research to include influence on society, business practice, and policy, not just academia." (2025 AACSB Research Impact Survey; deans n=188, faculty n=639; p. 12)
- "If the RRBM Principles provide the roadmap, the following Research Impact Ecosystem Guide serves as the engine." (p. 29)
- On where to start: "The task force believes that the most effective place for business schools to begin driving systemic change is within their own ecosystem, the sphere in which deans, associate deans, research directors, and faculty have the greatest direct influence." (p. 29)
- The dissent, quoted by the task force in its own report, from a survey respondent: "As academic institutions, impact should be predominantly based on academic impact … other factors (e.g., policy implications, attracting external funding, industry engagements) are BONUSES and natural outcomes when the academic research is academically sound … Extending the definition of 'impact' too broadly indeed requests academics to move away from their competitive edge … racing the discipline to the bottom in the long run."

## The Five Imperatives Assigned to AOM

The framework's "Call for Collective Action" gives every stakeholder group a list. Under **Academies and Scholarly Associations** (p. 36), the full list is:

1. **Norm-Setting and Advocacy:** champion a broader definition of research excellence that includes impact, influence, and engagement alongside traditional scholarly outputs.
2. **Community-Building:** create platforms (special interest groups, conferences, networks) connecting scholars interested in impact-oriented and interdisciplinary research.
3. **Recognition and Incentives:** introduce awards, grants, and recognition programs that elevate impactful research.
4. **Promotional Platforms:** develop partnerships with media, consultancies, and practitioner-focused organizations to promote member research.
5. **Capacity-Building:** provide training and resources to help scholars translate research into practice, engage external audiences, and navigate nontraditional dissemination channels.

That is the entire association-level guidance in the document: five bullets, roughly 120 words.

## What the Framework Does Not Say (Your White Space)

This is the most important section of this summary. The framework is written **for deans**. Business school leaders get a dense page of imperatives; accrediting bodies get a full page; associations get five bullets.

Three consequences follow, and they define this engagement:

- **The agency argument does not transfer.** The framework's core practical move is "start where you have agency," and the agency it names is the school's: promotion and tenure criteria, hiring, resource allocation, faculty development. AOM is not a school. It sets no tenure standard, hires no faculty, and allocates no research budget. The framework's central lever is one AOM does not hold.
- **The seven ecosystem levers are school-shaped.** Strategic Intent, Governance, Capacity and Incentives, Impact Portfolio, Activation Pathways, Measurement, Amplification. Whether these translate to an 18,000-member association, and what they would even mean, is unwritten.
- **The measurement tool has no association-level baseline.** The spiral in Appendix B tells a school what to count. Nobody has run it on a field, and AOM currently has no impact baseline of its own (see `data/`).

The task force said Phase 2 is implementation. For associations, Phase 2 is a blank page. That page is what the Board has engaged you to fill.

## Both/And Note

The framework is not a critique of rigor and neither is this engagement. It states plainly that rigor "remains foundational," and it prints its own dissenting voices at length rather than dismissing them. The report also names an unresolved risk in its own survey data: a minority of respondents warned that expanding the definition too far could dilute academic rigor. Any recommendation that fails to answer that objection has not met the standard the client's own document sets.
