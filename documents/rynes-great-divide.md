# Across the Great Divide

**Full citation:** Rynes, Bartunek & Daft, 2001, Across the Great Divide: Knowledge Creation and Transfer Between Practitioners and Academics, Academy of Management Journal 44(2): 340-355, doi 10.2307/3069460.

**Why it's in this data room:** This is the field's most systematic empirical statement of the academic-practitioner knowledge-transfer gap, written as the introduction to AMJ's Special Research Forum on the topic. It grounds the engagement's baseline question: is the low-impact critique fair, and how would we know? It also supplies the field's own diagnosis of why transfer fails, which any intervention must address.

## Core Argument

The authors argue that while the gap between organizational research and managerial practice is old news, nearly all commentary on it has been opinion rather than evidence, and that economic and institutional shifts (Bayh-Dole, corporate downsizing of research staffs, declining public funding) now push academics and practitioners toward each other. Drawing on Nonaka and Takeuchi's theory of knowledge creation, they diagnose the gap as structural: elite journals reward explicit-to-explicit knowledge combination, the conversion mode least connected to the tacit knowledge practitioners actually use, while socialization, externalization, and internalization are scarce in academic work. Knowledge transfer, they conclude, is fundamentally social; closing the gap requires deliberate institutional strategies, not better dissemination of the same outputs.

## Key Evidence & Quotes

- p. 340: "A substantial body of evidence suggests that executives typically do not turn to academics or academic research findings in developing management strategies and practices."
- p. 340: "Similarly, researchers rarely turn to practitioners for inspiration in setting their research questions (Sackett & Larson, 1990) or for insight in interpreting their results (Rynes, McNatt, & Bretz, 1999)."
- p. 341, quoting Beyer and Trice's review of research utilization: "the most persistent observation . . . is that researchers and users belong to separate communities with very different values and ideologies and that these differences impede utilization" (1982: 608).
- p. 343, on the forum's 49 submissions: "Coding revealed that fewer than 20 percent of the studies involved practitioners in their initial designs or engaged them as coauthors after the studies had been completed."
- p. 346: "Offermann and Spiros found that many practitioners—even those with doctoral degrees—stop reading academic journals once they enter the world of practice."
- p. 346: "transfer of knowledge has been shown to fail even when there is strong evidence of its superiority over prior understandings, when those transmitting and receiving the new knowledge are from the same organization, and when the target for the knowledge has great need of a solution and may even have commissioned a search for alternatives."
- p. 348: "the most elite organizational science journals are heavily dominated by explicit-to-explicit knowledge conversion, which is the form least directly associated with tacit knowledge."
- p. 350: "This sanitization of complexities and uncertainties is likely to make research findings less credible to practitioners who, after all, live and work in 'messy' worlds."

## Relevance to the Engagement

For the measurement baseline, the paper shows that as of 2001 the impact critique rested largely on anecdote, and it models real evidence: practitioner readership, citation in practice, and coded rates of practitioner involvement in research. For translation infrastructure, its four recommendations (embrace academic-practitioner tension, expand association-facilitated interaction, editorial support for diverse knowledge forms, broadened rhetorical strategies) are a ready-made option set the team can test and cost. For the education channel, the finding that even doctorally trained practitioners stop reading journals implies that classrooms and executive programs, not journal access, are the durable pipe from scholarship to practice.

## Both/And Note

The authors never propose relaxing rigor; their forum rejected most submissions precisely for weak construct validity and theory. Their claim is that submissions drawing on both academic and practitioner data were more likely to survive review, suggesting engagement and rigor are complements. This is the case's both/and thesis, stated empirically decades before the current debate.
