# Ghoshal: Bad Management Theories Are Destroying Good Management Practices

**Full citation:** Ghoshal, S. 2005. Bad management theories are destroying good management practices. *Academy of Management Learning & Education*, 4(1): 75–91. DOI: 10.5465/amle.2005.16132558.

**Why it's in this data room:** This is the strongest form of the impact critique: management scholarship does not merely fail to reach practice, its ideas can actively shape practice for the worse. If theories carry real-world consequences, the impact question becomes a matter of professional responsibility, raising the stakes of the Board's engagement.

## Core Argument

Ghoshal argues that many of the worst excesses of recent management practice have intellectual roots in business school research of the prior thirty years. Two trends combined: the "pretense of knowledge" (a scientistic model that excludes human intentionality and, with it, morality from theory) and an "ideology-based gloomy vision" built on pessimistic assumptions about people, embodied in agency theory, transaction cost economics, and the self-interest model. Because social theories are self-fulfilling through the "double hermeneutic," managers taught that people are opportunistic build controls that produce opportunism, and negative assumptions become realized pathologies. He shows that agency theory's governance prescriptions lack empirical support yet dominate, and that the field has crowded out Boyer's scholarships of integration, application, and pedagogy. His remedy: reengage those scholarships, re-legitimize pluralism, and rebuild theory on richer assumptions about human nature, a task only academics, deans, governing boards, and institutions like the Academy of Management can lead.

## Key Evidence & Quotes

- "Our theories and ideas have done much to strengthen the management practices that we are all now so loudly condemning." (p. 75)
- "By propagating ideologically inspired amoral theories, business schools have actively freed their students from any sense of moral responsibility." (p. 76)
- "A theory that draws prescriptions on corporate governance on the assumption that managers cannot be trusted can make managers less trustworthy" (p. 77)
- "What started off as an entirely justified effort for introducing the scholarship of discovery to the study of business has ended up in the excess of eliminating all other forms of scholarship from the world of business schools." (p. 82)
- "The obverse is also true: Nothing is as dangerous as a bad theory." (p. 86)
- "In essence, social scientists carry an even greater social and moral responsibility than those who work in the physical sciences because, if they hide ideology in the pretense of science, they can cause much more harm." (p. 87)
- "If we really wish to reinstitute ethical or moral concerns in the practice of management, we have to first reinstitute them in our mainstream theory." (p. 87)
- "I believe that as academics, we may have been guilty of overexploiting our freedom." (p. 88)

## Relevance to the Engagement

Ghoshal reframes impact from a dissemination problem into a content-and-incentives problem: what the field theorizes, and what its institutions reward, flows into practice whether or not anyone builds a communication channel. For AOM strategy, his closing pages are unusually operational. He assigns the Academy a named role: create "a new intellectual agenda," have senior scholars provide "air cover" for junior faculty who take risks, dedicate journal special issues to legitimize broader scholarship, and make the topic "the core theme of a forthcoming annual meeting" (p. 89). These are direct uses of AOM's convening power, incentive levers (editorial signals, tenure-adjacent legitimacy), and channels. He also points to deans, governing boards, and alumni as countervailing forces the Academy could mobilize as partners.

## Both/And Note

Ghoshal states explicitly that he does not "intend to imply either that we should abandon the effort to develop systematic theory in the field of management or that we should not study some of the more distasteful aspects of individual and organizational behavior" (p. 86), and he concedes scientific methods "undoubtedly yielded some significant benefits" (p. 77). His target is unbalanced assumptions hidden as science, not rigor itself: the prescription is pluralism and richer theory paired with disciplined scholarship, precisely the both/and pairing the engagement pursues.
