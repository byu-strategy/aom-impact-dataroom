# Akerlof: Sins of Omission and the Practice of Economics

**Full citation:** Akerlof, G. A. 2020. Sins of Omission and the Practice of Economics. *Journal of Economic Literature* 58(2): 405-418. doi:10.1257/jel.20191573.

**Why it's in this data room:** This is the Board's both/and charter text, written by a Nobel laureate about his own field. Akerlof does not attack rigor; he argues that over-rewarding "hardness" causes a discipline to omit important questions. It arrives from outside management, so it critiques the system without singling out anyone in this room.

## Core Argument

Economics rewards research that is "hard" (precise, quantified, causally identified, mathematically formalized) over research that is important but resistant to hard methods. Individually rational researchers therefore choose topics that are harder and less important than the social optimum, and in aggregate the profession leaves a set of important-but-soft topics unstudied: sins of omission. The bias is self-reinforcing through three mechanisms (status in the scientific pecking order, committee evaluation dynamics that agree easily on precision but disagree on importance, and selection of hard-taste people into the profession), and it produces bias against new ideas, overspecialization, and the "curse of the top five" in tenure decisions. His remedy is institutional: reexamine publication and promotion norms, in the spirit of medicine's Flexner Report.

## Key Evidence & Quotes

- "economics, as a discipline, gives rewards that favor the 'hard' and disfavor the 'soft.' Such bias leads economic research to ignore important topics and problems that are difficult to approach in a 'hard' way—thereby resulting in 'sins of omission.'" (p. 405)
- "His chosen topic (topic A) is both harder and less important than the social optimum (topic B)." (p. 406)
- "Precision is a relatively well-defined concept; hence, it is easy for people to agree regarding the hardness/softness of research. In contrast, importance is fuzzy, so that it is relatively easy to disagree regarding its importance." (p. 406)
- "a new idea is less well-formulated than … well-established ideas and therefore rejected for lack of rigor." (p. 408, quoting Frey 2003)
- "tenure and promotion committees are increasingly relying upon journal metrics to make decisions, with the number of 'top five' publications given particular weight." (p. 409)
- "only 3 percent of economists thought it 'very important' for their success to 'have a thorough knowledge of the economy'; in contrast 65 percent thought it 'very important' to be 'smart in the sense of being good at problem solving.'" (p. 411, citing Colander and Klamer 1987)
- On the 2008 crisis: "There were incentives to present the key pieces of the puzzle, but none to put them together." (p. 412)
- "The theoretical and empirical accomplishments of modern economics, obtained with hard standards for the conduct of research, should be rightly celebrated. But such standards should not be uniformly applied to all economic problems." (p. 416)
- "Different terrains call for different vehicles. A sailboat is useless in crossing a (riverless) desert; a camel is useless in crossing a sea." (p. 416)

## The Management Analog

Substitute "theoretical contribution" for "hardness" and the model transfers almost without modification. Management's committees also agree easily on methodological rigor and theoretical positioning while disagreeing on practical importance, so evaluation converges on what is checkable rather than what matters; gap-spotting incrementalism (Alvesson & Sandberg 2011) is the field's version of topic A over topic B. The crisis example has a direct management parallel: the field's most important current phenomenon, AI's transformation of work and organizations, is being studied fastest by economists and computer scientists while management's contribution lags (see `impact-debate.md`), a live sin of omission in the making. Akerlof's remedy list (examine journal norms, editor and referee conduct, and promotion criteria including metric-dependence) reads as a ready-made scope for this engagement's issue tree.

## Both/And Note

Akerlof explicitly celebrates what hard standards achieved and never proposes lowering them; his target is their uniform application to every problem. That is precisely the Board's mandate: keep the rigor, stop letting it define the boundary of what counts as a contribution, and recover the important questions the current optimization leaves out.
